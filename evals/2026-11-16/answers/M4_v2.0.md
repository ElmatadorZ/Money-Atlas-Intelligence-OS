**Nominal vs. real interest rates**

The **nominal rate** is the rate you see quoted: on a savings account, a bond, a loan. It tells you how many more units of money you get back.

The **real rate** is the nominal rate after inflation is taken out. It tells you how much more you can actually buy with that money.

**The relationship (Fisher equation)**

- Approximation: **Real ≈ Nominal − Inflation**
- Exact: **(1 + Real) = (1 + Nominal) / (1 + Inflation)**

**Example (made-up numbers):**
- A deposit pays 5% nominal and inflation is 3%. Your real return is about **+2%**. Your money grows faster than prices.
- A deposit pays 2% nominal and inflation is 4%. Your real return is about **−2%**. You have more baht or dollars, but you can buy less with them.

---

📍 **CORE INSIGHT: why the distinction matters**

- **Savers and investors:** the real rate is your actual gain or loss in purchasing power. A high nominal yield during high inflation can still mean you are getting poorer.
- **Borrowers:** inflation lowers the real cost of a fixed-rate debt, because you repay in money that is worth less.
- **Markets:** many asset prices respond to *real* rates more than nominal ones. Gold, growth stocks, and long-duration assets are all sensitive to real yields. When real rates rise, holding assets that pay no yield costs more. When real rates fall or turn negative, it costs less.
- **Central banks:** monetary policy is "tight" or "loose" in real terms. A 5% policy rate with 2% inflation is restrictive. The same 5% with 7% inflation is still loose.

📍 **KEY RISK: where people get it wrong**

- **Inflation is not known in advance.** The rate you lock in is nominal, but your real return depends on inflation *over the period you hold it*. The **ex-ante** (expected) real rate can differ a lot from the **ex-post** (realized) one if inflation surprises.
- **Which inflation measure?** Headline CPI, core CPI, or your own personal cost of living can give different real rates.
- **Taxes.** Tax applies to the nominal return, so your after-tax real return can be lower than the simple formula suggests.
- One market-based way to see expected real rates is inflation-linked bonds (such as US TIPS). Their yield is roughly a real yield. The gap between a normal bond yield and the inflation-linked yield (the "breakeven") is the inflation rate the market expects. That expectation can be wrong too.

📍 **STRATEGIC TAKEAWAY**

Judge any yield, loan, or policy rate in real terms, not nominal terms: **nominal minus expected inflation**. A return is only a gain if it beats inflation, and real rates, not headline rates, are what drive the pricing of savings, debt, and most major asset classes.
