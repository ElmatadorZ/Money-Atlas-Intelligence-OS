**Short answer: not yet.** A 300% annual return with a 2% max drawdown almost always means something is wrong with the backtest, not that you've found a money machine. Treat it as a bug to find before you treat it as an edge. The decision is yours, but here is the framework I'd use to make it.

Here is what I actually know: two numbers, +300% per year and a 2% max drawdown. I don't know the asset, the timeframe, the test period, the number of trades, the cost model, how many parameters you tuned, or whether any of it was tested on data the bot never saw.

📍 **FIRST PRINCIPLE BREAKDOWN**

- **Problem:** Is the result you measured the result you'll get with real money?
- **Cause:** A backtest is a simulation, and its output depends entirely on what it assumes. A return-to-drawdown ratio of about 150:1 is far beyond what real-money strategies usually show over time. When a simulation looks this good, the usual reason is that it assumes something the live market won't give you.
- **Mechanism.** These are the usual ways a backtest makes a result this good:
  1. **Look-ahead bias.** The bot uses information it wouldn't have had at the time: the close of a candle it trades on, indicators that repaint, data that was revised later, or signals from higher timeframes that weren't finished yet.
  2. **Overfitting.** The parameters were tuned until the curve looked perfect. The bot learned the noise in your test data, not a pattern in the market.
  3. **Fill and cost fantasy.** No spread, no slippage, no commission or swap, fills at the exact limit price or at the high or low of a bar, or tick data that's been interpolated. Scalping and high-frequency strategies are very sensitive to this.
  4. **Hidden tail risk.** Martingale, grid, averaging down, or positions with no stop loss. These show a tiny drawdown right up until one move wipes out the account. On a closed-trade equity curve, a 2% drawdown can hide huge open losses while trades are running.
  5. **Too little data or a single market regime.** A short test window, or one long trend, with too few trades to mean anything.
  6. **Survivorship and data quality.** Delisted assets are missing, there are gaps in the data, or the data comes from a broker whose feed differs from the one you'll trade on.
- **Leverage point:** The asymmetry is all on your side if you check before you deploy. Testing costs you time. Skipping it can cost you the account.
- **Outcome:** See the scenarios below.

📍 **SYSTEM MAP**

Here is the chain from backtest to live money, and where each link can break:

Backtest assumptions → historical data quality → strategy logic → execution model (spread, slippage, latency, partial fills) → the regime the market is in now → your broker and infrastructure → live P&L

Live results can only be as good as the weakest link. Right now you've only tested the first three, inside a simulation.

📍 **NARRATIVE INTELLIGENCE**

The story you want to believe is "I found an edge nobody else has." The likelier story is "the simulator is being generous." For scale: firms with far more data, speed and capital consider a return-to-drawdown ratio in the low single digits excellent. That doesn't prove your bot is broken. It does mean the burden of proof is very high.

📍 **SCENARIOS**

🐻 **Bear (most common for numbers like these): it's a backtest artifact.**
- **Trigger:** One of the six mechanisms above is present.
- **Outcome:** Live results fall apart quickly, or slowly bleed out, or one tail event blows up the account (if it's martingale or grid).
- **Condition:** The results vanish once you add realistic costs, run it on out-of-sample data, or shift the parameters slightly.

⚖️ **Base (most probable if the logic is sound): there's a real edge, but it's much smaller.**
- **Outcome:** After costs, slippage and a regime change, live returns come in far below the backtest and drawdowns are several times larger.
- **Condition:** It survives an out-of-sample test with clearly worse numbers, but still has a positive expectancy.
- That can still be worth trading, but at a size that fits the real drawdown, not the 2% one.

🐂 **Bull (rare): the edge is real and mostly holds up.**
- **Condition:** It survives every test below, including live trading with a small amount of capital that roughly matches the backtest over a meaningful number of trades.
- **Even then:** Edges decay. Plan to monitor it and set a rule for when to switch it off.

⚡ **Chaos: an infrastructure failure.**
- The edge is real, but the live system breaks: the VPS goes down, the connection to the broker drops, orders get rejected, the spread widens during news, or there's a weekend gap. This is unrelated to the strategy and can still cause real losses.

📍 **DECISION FRAMEWORK: tests to run before any real money**

1. **Audit for look-ahead.** Make sure every signal uses only bars that have already closed. Check for repainting indicators and for trades entered at prices that weren't available yet.
2. **Use realistic costs.** Use your broker's real spread (including the widening during news and rollover), plus slippage, commission and swap. In MT5, use "Every tick based on real ticks" mode, not OHLC or open prices only. Rerun the test. If the edge disappears, stop there.
3. **Test out of sample.** Hold back data the bot was never tuned on, ideally from a different market regime, or run a walk-forward test.
4. **Test parameter robustness.** Change each parameter by ±10–20%. A real edge degrades gradually. An overfit one falls off a cliff.
5. **Count trades and check statistics.** You want enough trades (at least hundreds, as a rough rule) and a look at the distribution. Is the profit coming from a handful of outlier trades? Run a Monte Carlo reshuffle of trade order to see a realistic range of drawdowns.
6. **Check the risk structure.** Is there a hard stop on every position? Any martingale, grid or averaging down? What is the worst floating (open) drawdown, not just the closed-trade drawdown?
7. **Forward test on a demo account** for weeks to months.
8. **Go live small.** Use money you can afford to lose entirely. Compare live fills and slippage with the backtest trade by trade.
9. **Scale up gradually,** and only while live results stay within the range your tests predicted.

How this depends on your situation:
- **If your timeframe is short (scalping or M1–M5):** Execution realism (step 2) matters most. That's where these strategies usually die.
- **If your risk tolerance is low:** Don't move past step 7 until you have months of demo data.
- **If you can't explain why the edge exists** (who is on the losing side of your trades, and why they keep losing): treat that as a warning sign whatever the numbers say.

📍 **RISK & FAILURE MODE**

- **The biggest risk:** You size your position for a 2% drawdown and then face a real drawdown of 20–50% or more.
- **Second:** A martingale or grid structure that looks perfectly smooth until it suddenly isn't.
- **This whole analysis is wrong if:** The bot already passes realistic-cost, out-of-sample, walk-forward and forward tests. Then the base or bull case applies and the question becomes how to size it, not whether to deploy it.
