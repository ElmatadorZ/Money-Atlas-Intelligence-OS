## Why "is now a good time?" is mostly the wrong question for DCA

DCA (dollar-cost averaging) means investing a fixed amount on a fixed schedule, whatever the price. You use it because you can't time the market. If you hold off until the time feels right, you're timing the market again.

- **Over long periods, starting dates have mattered less than staying in.** The S&P 500 has gone up over most 10+ year periods, but not all of them. Someone who started in 2000 or 2007 had a rough first decade.
- **A drop after you start helps a DCA investor.** Your later contributions buy more shares at lower prices.
- **The real risk is stopping.** Most DCA plans fail because the person pauses or sells during a 20–30% drawdown, not because they started on a bad date.

## What actually decides whether it's a good time *for you*

1. **Time horizon:** If you won't need this money for 7–10+ years, starting now is reasonable. If you need it within 1–3 years, it shouldn't be in stocks at all.
2. **Emergency fund:** Have 3–6 months of expenses in cash first, so a job loss or emergency doesn't force you to sell at a low.
3. **High-interest debt:** Paying off credit-card-type debt usually beats expected stock returns.
4. **Can you stomach a drawdown?** The S&P 500 has fallen 30–50% several times. If seeing your portfolio down a third would make you sell, start with a smaller allocation.

## A note on valuation

US large-cap valuations have been high by historical standards for much of recent years, and the index has become heavily concentrated in a few mega-cap tech stocks. Historically, high starting valuations have tended to mean **lower average returns over the following 10 years**, not a crash on a predictable date. That's a reason to keep expectations modest and maybe diversify a bit, not a reason to stay out entirely.

## Practical points if you're investing from Thailand

- **Currency risk:** Your returns depend on USD/THB as well as the index. A stronger baht can eat into your gains, and a weaker one adds to them. Some Thai S&P 500 funds hedge the currency and some don't, so check which kind you're buying.
- **How you buy:** Your choices include Thai feeder funds that track the S&P 500 (easy to automate, possibly tax-advantaged through SSF/RMF, but with higher fees) or buying a low-cost ETF like VOO, IVV, CSPX or SPLG through an offshore broker (lower fees, but more steps and possible tax and FX costs). Compare the **total cost**: expense ratio, FX spread, and transfer fees.
- **Tax:** Rules on foreign-sourced income have changed in recent years, so confirm how your gains will be taxed when you bring money back to Thailand.

## A simple way to start

- Choose an amount you can keep up through a bad year, such as 10–20% of monthly savings.
- Automate it monthly so you aren't deciding each time.
- Decide in advance what you'll do in a 30% drop. Ideally that's "keep going," or "add more" if you have spare cash.
- Review once or twice a year, not every day.

**Bottom line:** If your horizon is long, your emergency fund is in place, and you can keep contributing through a downturn, any month is a fine time to start DCA. Waiting for a better entry tends to cost people more than a bad entry does. If any of those conditions aren't true yet, sort them out first.
