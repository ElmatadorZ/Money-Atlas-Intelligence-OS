**Short answer: a debt-driven crash is possible, but it is not the most likely path. The bigger risk is the dollar slowly losing value over years, not collapsing.**

📍 **MARKET STRUCTURE INSIGHT**
The question assumes high debt leads to a crash. That link is weaker than it sounds. The US borrows in its own currency, so it will not run out of dollars to pay debts the way Argentina or Greece did. The real channels are inflation (paying debts with weaker dollars) and investors asking for higher interest to keep lending.

"Crash" can mean two things. It can mean falling against other currencies, but the euro, yen and yuan come with debt and growth problems of their own. Or it can mean losing purchasing power, which is already happening slowly.

Last known context (from my training data, as of mid-2026, may have changed): federal debt was above $38 trillion, and yearly interest costs were around $1 trillion. In the first half of 2025 the dollar index fell about 10%. That was a sharp drop, but not a collapse. What I cannot see from here: today's exchange rates, Treasury yields or auction results.

📍 **KEY RISK**
The danger signal is the dollar falling *while* long-term Treasury yields jump. That combination means foreign buyers are stepping away from US assets. A second danger signal is the Fed being pushed to hold rates down to fund the government. Either one would make a sharp fall far more likely.

📍 **STRATEGIC TAKEAWAY**
- **Slow erosion (most likely):** deficits stay large, inflation runs a bit above target, the dollar's share of global reserves keeps slipping. *Trigger:* deficits near 6% of GDP with no crisis.
- **Sharp break (less likely):** weak Treasury auctions, yields and the dollar falling together, and the Fed's independence in doubt. *Trigger:* that pattern lasting for weeks, not days.
- **Dollar stays strong:** the US economy keeps outgrowing its peers, or a global shock sends money into dollars for safety.

**Invalidation:** this view is wrong if the "yields up, dollar down" pattern becomes persistent rather than episodic.

**CONFIDENCE: MEDIUM.** The basis is structural: the US borrows in its own currency, the dollar's reserve role has no large-scale substitute, and dominant reserve currencies have historically declined over decades. I cannot put a probability on the timing.
