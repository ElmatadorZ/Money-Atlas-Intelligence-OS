Bond prices fall when yields rise. The two always move in opposite directions.

**Why:** A bond's coupon is fixed when it is issued. If market yields go up after that, new bonds pay more than your bond does. The only way your bond can offer a buyer the same return as a new one is to sell for less. The lower price makes up for the smaller coupon. When yields fall, the same logic works the other way and the price goes up.

**Example:** You hold a 10-year bond with a face value of $1,000 and a 3% coupon, so it pays $30 a year. Market yields then rise to 4%, and new bonds pay $40 a year. Nobody will pay $1,000 for your $30 a year, so your bond's price drops until its total return matches 4%. For a 10-year bond, that is about $919.

**How big the move is depends on duration.** The longer a bond has until it matures, and the lower its coupon, the more its price changes when yields move. A 1-point rise in yields barely moves a 1-year bill. It moves a 30-year bond a lot.

**Why it matters:** The price drop is a loss only if you sell before the bond matures. If you hold it to maturity and the issuer doesn't default, you still get the full face value back. Bond funds never mature, though, so when rates rise they show the loss directly.
