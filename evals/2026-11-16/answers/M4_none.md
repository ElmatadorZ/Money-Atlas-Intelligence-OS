**Nominal interest rate** is the rate you see quoted, such as the rate on a savings account, a loan or a bond. It tells you how much your money grows in currency terms.

**Real interest rate** is the nominal rate minus inflation. It tells you how much your **purchasing power** grows, meaning how much more you can actually buy.

**The basic formula (a good approximation):**

> Real rate ≈ Nominal rate − Inflation rate

**Example:**
- Your savings account pays **5%** a year (nominal).
- Inflation is **3%**.
- Your real return is about **2%**. You have 5% more money, but things cost 3% more, so you can buy only about 2% more than before.

If inflation is **6%** while the account pays 5%, your real rate is about **−1%**. Your balance goes up, but your money buys less than it did a year ago.

**The exact formula** (this matters when rates or inflation are high):

> (1 + nominal) = (1 + real) × (1 + inflation)
> Real = (1 + nominal) / (1 + inflation) − 1

With 5% nominal and 3% inflation: 1.05 / 1.03 − 1 ≈ **1.94%**.

**Why the difference matters:**
- **Savers and investors:** The real rate shows whether your wealth is actually growing. A high nominal rate means little if inflation is higher.
- **Borrowers:** Inflation erodes the real value of debt. A fixed-rate loan becomes cheaper in real terms when inflation rises.
- **Central banks and the economy:** Real rates drive spending and investment decisions. Low or negative real rates encourage borrowing, and high real rates tighten conditions.

**One nuance:** People sometimes separate *ex ante* real rates, which use expected inflation and are what lenders and borrowers plan around, from *ex post* real rates, which use the inflation that actually happened.

**In short:** the nominal rate is how much more money you get, and the real rate is how much more you can actually buy with it.
