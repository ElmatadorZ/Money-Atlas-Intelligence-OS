**Short answer: when yields rise, bond prices fall.** They move in opposite directions, and that follows from the arithmetic, not from market opinion.

📍 MARKET STRUCTURE INSIGHT

**Why it works this way:**
- **Problem:** An existing bond pays a fixed coupon. That payment does not change when market rates do.
- **Cause:** If new bonds start paying more (yields rise), nobody will pay full price for your older, lower-paying bond.
- **Mechanism:** The price drops until the old bond's fixed payments give a buyer the same return as the new, higher market yield. Price is just the present value of future cash flows, and a higher discount rate means a lower present value.
- **Outcome:** Price and yield are two sides of one equation. One goes up, the other goes down.

**Worked example (illustrative, not a live price):**
A 10-year bond with a 4% coupon, priced at 100 when market yields are 4%:
- Yields rise to **5%** → price falls to about **92.3** (roughly −7.7%)
- Yields fall to **3%** → price rises to about **108.5**

**How much the price moves depends on:**
| Factor | Effect when yields rise |
|---|---|
| **Duration / maturity** | The longer the bond, the bigger the drop. Rough rule: % price change ≈ −duration × change in yield. A bond with a duration of 8 loses about 8% for a +1% move in yield. |
| **Coupon size** | Lower-coupon bonds are more sensitive. Zero-coupon bonds are the most sensitive of all. |
| **Convexity** | The relationship is curved, not a straight line. Prices fall a little less when yields rise than they gain when yields fall by the same amount. |

**System view, from macro down to price:**
Fed policy / inflation expectations / term premium → market yields → discount rate → bond price. Short-term bonds track the Fed most closely. Long-term bonds react more to inflation expectations, government debt supply, and the term premium.

📍 KEY RISK

These cases change what the move means for you:
- **Why yields are rising matters.**
  - 🐻 *Inflation-driven:* the worst case for bonds. Real returns get eroded as well as prices falling.
  - ⚖️ *Growth-driven:* price losses are partly offset by a healthier economy, and stocks may hold up.
  - 🐻 *Term-premium or supply-driven:* long bonds get hit hardest, and the usual bond/stock diversification can break down. In 2022, both fell together.
- **Holding to maturity vs. selling:** the price drop is a mark-to-market loss. If you hold an individual bond to maturity and the issuer doesn't default, you still get par (100) back. Bond *funds* have no maturity date, so their losses are only recovered over time through higher reinvested income.
- **Credit risk is a separate thing.** Corporate bond prices can also fall because spreads widen, even when government yields stay flat.

📍 STRATEGIC TAKEAWAY

Rising yields hurt the bonds you already own, and the longer their duration, the more they hurt. The same rise also means new money gets better income going forward. The question to ask isn't "will I lose?" but "what is my duration, compared with how long I plan to hold?"

**Decision frame:**
- IF your holding period is **at or beyond** the bond's maturity → the price swings along the way matter much less. Higher yields mean better reinvestment.
- IF you may need to **sell before maturity** → keep duration short. Price risk is the real risk for you.
