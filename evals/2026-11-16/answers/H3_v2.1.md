**Short answer:** DCA is designed so you don't need a "good time." If your horizon is long and your base is solid, the best time to start a DCA plan is when you have the money and the discipline to keep going, not when the market looks cheap.

📍 **MARKET STRUCTURE INSIGHT**
- **Premise check:** "Is now a good time?" is a timing question, and DCA exists to take timing off the table. You buy a fixed amount on a schedule, so you get more shares when prices fall and fewer when they rise. Whether this month is a "good entry" matters much less than whether you can keep buying for 10+ years.
- **Known:** The S&P 500 has had long-run gains along with deep drawdowns, some of which took years to recover. DCA does not prevent losses. It spreads out your entry risk. One relevant comparison: Vanguard's research found that putting a lump sum in at once has historically beaten DCA more often than not, because markets rise more often than they fall. *[UNVERIFIED here, so check the study; the exact frequency depends on the period.]* DCA's real job is to manage regret and behaviour, not to boost returns.
- **Unknown from here:** today's index level, valuation and rates (I have no live data, and my information stops around mid-2026), plus your finances.

📍 **KEY RISK**
The main risk isn't your entry price. It's **stopping**: pausing or selling during a 20–40% drawdown, which is exactly when DCA does the most for you. If you can't commit to buying through a bear market, the plan doesn't hold up.

📍 **STRATEGIC TAKEAWAY**
- **Scenario A, market keeps rising:** your early buys do best. A lump sum would have done better still. The trigger is earnings growth staying ahead of valuation.
- **Scenario B, a drawdown comes soon:** your later buys come in at lower prices and your average cost falls. You only get that benefit if you keep buying. The trigger is a recession, earnings cuts or a rate shock.

Neither scenario breaks DCA. What does break it: you need the money within about 3–5 years, you have no emergency fund, or you're paying off high-interest debt. Then the order should be emergency fund → debt → DCA.

**To make this specific to you, tell me:** your time horizon, whether you have 3–6 months of expenses saved, any high-interest debt, and whether you'd be investing a lump sum or monthly income. If it's monthly income, DCA is simply how you invest. If it's a lump sum, the real choice is lump sum vs. spreading it over 6–12 months.

Keep each contribution an amount you could keep investing through a bad year. Don't use leverage, and don't use money you'll need soon. The decision is yours. For advice tailored to your country's tax accounts, talk to a licensed adviser.

**CONFIDENCE: HIGH** that DCA in a broad index suits long-horizon investors. That rests on how DCA works and on the index's long-run history. **Not assessable** for whether this month is a good entry, because I have no live valuation or price data.
