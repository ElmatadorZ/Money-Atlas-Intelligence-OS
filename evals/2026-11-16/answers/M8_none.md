**Bond prices fall when yields rise.** They move in opposite directions, like two ends of a seesaw.

**Why it works this way**

A bond pays a fixed coupon. Say you hold a bond that pays 3% a year. Then market yields climb, and new bonds now pay 5%. Nobody will pay full price for your 3% bond when a 5% one is available. The only way to sell yours is to lower its price until a buyer's total return comes out close to 5%.

**A simple example**

- A 10-year bond with a $1,000 face value pays a $30 coupon each year (3%).
- Market yields rise to 4%.
- The bond's price drops to roughly **$919**. At that price, the $30 coupons plus the $1,000 you get back at maturity work out to about 4% a year.

It works the other way too. If yields fall, existing bonds with higher coupons become more valuable, and their prices rise.

**How much the price moves depends on:**

1. **Time to maturity (duration).** Longer bonds are hit harder. A 1% rise in yields might cut a 2-year bond's price by about 2%, but a 30-year bond's price by 15–20% or more.
2. **Coupon size.** Bonds with lower coupons, and zero-coupon bonds most of all, are more sensitive.
3. **Convexity.** The relationship is curved, not a straight line. Prices fall a bit less when yields rise than they gain when yields fall by the same amount.

**What it means for you**

- **If you hold to maturity**, price changes along the way don't matter much. You still collect every coupon and get the full face value back, as long as the issuer doesn't default.
- **If you might sell early, or you own a bond fund**, rising yields mean losses on paper, and real losses if you sell. Bond funds never "mature," so this risk is always there.
- **For new buyers**, higher yields are good news, because you lock in more income.

**Rule of thumb:** Price change ≈ −Duration × Change in yield. A bond with a duration of 7 loses about 7% when yields rise by 1 percentage point.
