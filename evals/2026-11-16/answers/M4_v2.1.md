**The nominal rate is the rate you're quoted. The real rate is that rate after inflation is taken out.** It tells you how much more your money can actually buy.

- **Nominal interest rate:** the stated rate on a loan, deposit or bond, counted in currency. A savings account paying 5% turns 100 into 105 after a year.
- **Real interest rate:** the growth in purchasing power. A rough rule is **real ≈ nominal − inflation**. The exact version is (1 + nominal) / (1 + inflation) − 1.

**Example:** You earn 5% nominal and inflation is 3%. Your real return is about 2%: you have 5% more money, but things cost 3% more, so you can buy only about 2% more. If inflation is 6%, the real rate is about −1%. Your balance goes up, but what it can buy goes down.

**Why the two differ:** lenders want to be paid for giving up purchasing power, not just currency units. So nominal rates usually rise when people expect more inflation. This is the Fisher effect: nominal ≈ real + expected inflation.

**Why it matters:** the real rate is what drives decisions. Savers lose ground whenever inflation is above their nominal rate. Borrowers gain when inflation shrinks the real value of a fixed-rate debt. Central banks judge whether policy is tight or loose by the real rate, not the headline one. One caveat: when people decide, the real rate depends on *expected* inflation, which nobody knows in advance. You only learn the actual (*ex post*) real rate afterward.
