**Short answer:** Whether "now" is a good time can't be known in advance, and DCA exists so you don't have to know. The better question is whether DCA suits your money and your timeline.

📍 **MARKET STRUCTURE INSIGHT**
- **The premise:** "good time" is a market-timing question. Nobody can reliably call the next 6 to 12 months of the S&P 500, and a regular schedule makes the entry date matter less.
- **What I know:** my data ends in mid-2026. Up to then, US large caps were trading at valuations above their long-run average. That has historically pointed to lower long-run returns, but it has told you little about the next year. I don't have today's level or valuation, so check those yourself.
- **The real comparison:** if you already have a lump sum, investing it all at once has usually beaten spreading it out. Vanguard's 2012 study of US data from 1926 to 2011 found this in about two-thirds of periods, because markets rise more often than they fall. DCA's value is behavioural: it caps your regret if a crash comes right after you start. If the money comes from your monthly income, DCA is simply investing as you earn, and that's sound at any market level.

📍 **KEY RISK**
The plan fails if you stop buying or sell during a large drawdown. The S&P has fallen by roughly half more than once, most recently in 2008–09. It also fails if you need the money within about 5 years.

📍 **STRATEGIC TAKEAWAY**
- **Market falls after you start:** your later buys come in cheaper and DCA beats a lump sum. This only pays off if you keep buying.
- **Market keeps rising:** DCA trails a lump sum, and that gap is the price of lower regret.

Either way, the things that decide this are personal:
- a horizon of 10 years or more
- an emergency fund already in place
- no high-interest debt
- low-cost index fund fees
- an amount you can keep paying through a bad year
