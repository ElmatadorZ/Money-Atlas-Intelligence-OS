**Short answer: if you lever 2x, expect about 10% a year, not 15%, with roughly twice the volatility and a worst drawdown somewhere around half your money. Borrowing costs and volatility drag eat most of the extra return. At a realistic borrowing rate, no amount of leverage gets this portfolio to 15%.**

📍 SITUATION MAP
- **Given:** 8% a year, 10% volatility. I'm treating the 8% as a compound (CAGR) figure.
- **Premise check:** that 8% is a *past* number. A large part of it came from about four decades of falling interest rates, which helped both stocks and bonds. In 2022, as far as my data goes, stocks and bonds fell together and a 60/40 lost in the mid-to-high teens. Leverage would multiply whatever the *future* return is, not the past one.
- **Unknown:** your borrowing rate, how you would lever (margin, futures or a leveraged fund), your time horizon, and what the 15% is for.

📍 THE ARITHMETIC
Inputs: borrowing cost **5% [ASSUMED]**, roughly a cash rate plus a broker spread. Check your actual rate.

1. Average annual return: 8% + (10%)²/2 ≈ **8.5%**
2. Levered 2x: 2 × 8.5% − 1 × 5% (interest on the borrowed half) = **12%**
3. Volatility doubles to **20%**, so volatility drag = (20%)²/2 = **2%**
4. Expected compound return ≈ 12% − 2% = **~10% a year**

What the borrowing rate does:

| Borrow cost | 2x compound return | Leverage needed for 15% |
|---|---|---|
| 0% | ~15% | 2x |
| 3% | ~12% | ~3x (vol 30%) |
| 5% | ~10% | **not reachable**. The best possible is ~11%, at ~3.5x |

Past about 3.5x, volatility drag rises faster than the extra return. At a 5% borrowing rate, 15% is out of reach at any leverage. That's the key finding.

📍 DRAWDOWN — what to expect
- **A normal year at 2x** (20% vol, assumed roughly normal): about **−8% to +32%** two years in three. About one year in twenty comes in **worse than about −20%**.
- **A crisis on the scale of 2008:** an unlevered 60/40 fell roughly a third peak to trough (approximate, last known). At 2x that becomes about **−50% to −60%**, depending on whether you rebalance the leverage. You would then need about +100% or more just to get back to where you started.
- **Margin call risk:** at 2x with a 25% maintenance requirement, a fall of about **33%** in the assets triggers a margin call: 200 in assets on a 100 loan → 133. That is roughly the size of the 2008 fall. A forced sale near the bottom turns a temporary drawdown into a permanent loss. This is the most serious risk here.

📍 SCENARIOS
- **Base (rates stay near current levels):** about 9–10% compound with a 20% vol ride. You add roughly 1–2 points a year over unlevered and take on twice the risk.
- **Good (returns hold at 8% and borrowing gets cheap):** you get close to 13–15%. This needs borrowing costs near zero, as in the 2010s.
- **Bad (stocks and bonds fall together while rates rise, like 2022):** you lose about twice as much, your borrowing cost goes up at the same moment, and a margin call becomes possible.

**What would change this view:** a borrowing rate below about 3%, or leverage that can't be called away from you (for example futures held with plenty of spare cash, or long-dated borrowing).

📍 DECISION FRAMEWORK
- **If you need 15%:** first check *why*. Saving more, working longer or accepting 8–10% usually costs less than the risk of leverage. Leverage is not a safe way to close a gap in a plan.
- **If you want more return and can stomach the swings:** raising your stock share (up to 100%) gets you more growth with no loan, no margin call and no interest. Size the position so a 50% drawdown wouldn't force you to sell.
- **If you still want leverage:** keep it modest (around 1.3–1.5x), use your real borrowing rate, keep cash outside the account to meet margin calls, and decide your rules for cutting it *before* a crash.

I won't recommend 2x leverage. The decision is yours, and a licensed adviser can look at your full situation.

**CONFIDENCE:** HIGH on the mechanics (the leverage math and the margin-call threshold are arithmetic). LOW on the actual outcome, because it depends on future returns and borrowing rates that nobody can know.
**KEY UNKNOWNS:** your borrowing rate, how you would lever, your time horizon, what the 15% is for, and how much of a fall you can survive without being forced to sell.
