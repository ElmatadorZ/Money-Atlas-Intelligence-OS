I can't help you trade on this. What you're describing is insider trading.

**The problem with the trade itself**

Your friend's information is material, because a big earnings miss moves the stock. It is also non-public, because it hasn't been announced yet. Trading on it is illegal in essentially every major market, including the US, UK, EU, Thailand and most others. That's true whichever instrument you'd pick: selling shares, shorting, buying puts, or using a spouse's or friend's account. Passing the tip on to someone else who trades counts too. Your friend may already have broken the law and company policy just by telling you.

This isn't a technicality with low odds of getting caught. Regulators and exchanges run surveillance specifically on trading before earnings. Options trades placed just before a big earnings move are among the patterns they look for most. Employees of the company are the first people they check. The usual results are:

- Losing your profits plus civil penalties that can be a multiple of the gain
- A criminal record and possible prison time
- Being fired, and for your friend too, since a case against you usually exposes your source

The exact penalties vary by country and change over time. Your local securities regulator lists the current ones.

**Why it isn't even a good bet**

Look at it purely as risk and reward. The upside is one trade's profit. The downside is open-ended: legal costs, penalties, your job and your record. The information may not even be right. Early numbers change, and the market may already expect a weak quarter. A big edge on paper becomes a small, uncertain gain against an outcome that can ruin you.

**What to do instead**

1. **Don't trade the company's stock or options** until the results are public and your company's trading window reopens. As an employee you're probably under a blackout period around earnings anyway.
2. **Don't pass the information on** to anyone, including family.
3. **Check your company's insider trading policy.** If you're unsure what you're allowed to do, ask the compliance or legal team. A question like "can I trade before earnings?" is routine for them.
4. **If you already hold the stock,** don't sell based on this tip. Changing your position now on this information is the same problem as opening a new one. Ask compliance or a lawyer how your company treats existing holdings during blackouts.
5. **If you're worried about how much of your money is in your employer's stock,** that's a real and fair concern. Deal with it through a pre-planned, approved route, such as a scheduled selling plan your company allows, during an open window.
