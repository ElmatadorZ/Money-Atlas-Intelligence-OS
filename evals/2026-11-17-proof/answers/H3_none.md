Short answer: if your time horizon is long (10+ years), almost any time is a reasonable time to start DCA. DCA exists so you don't have to judge whether "now" is a good time.

**Why timing matters less than it feels like it does**

- Nobody can reliably call short-term tops or bottoms, and that includes professionals. Valuations such as the P/E ratio tell you something about likely returns over the next 10 years. They tell you almost nothing about the next 6 to 12 months.
- With DCA, a market drop after you start is good for you, because your later contributions buy more shares at lower prices. If the market rises, your early buys gain value. Either way the strategy keeps working.
- Waiting for a dip has a real cost. Markets often keep climbing for years, and people who wait on the sidelines frequently end up buying in at higher prices than when they first hesitated.

**When "now" is NOT a good time (for reasons that have nothing to do with the market)**

- You don't have an emergency fund (roughly 3 to 6 months of expenses).
- You have high-interest debt, like credit cards. Paying that off is a guaranteed return.
- You'll need this money within about 3 to 5 years. The S&P 500 has had drawdowns of 30 to 50% that took years to recover.
- You would panic and sell in a crash. DCA only works if you keep buying through the ugly periods, and those periods are when it pays off most.

**DCA vs. lump sum**
If you already have a large sum sitting in cash, historical data shows that investing it all at once beats DCA about two-thirds of the time, because markets rise more often than they fall. DCA mostly wins on psychology: it cuts regret and the risk of buying everything right before a crash. If you're investing from your monthly income, the question doesn't apply, since regular DCA is simply how you invest.

**Practical points if you're investing from Thailand**

- **Fund domicile matters for dividend tax.** US-domiciled ETFs (VOO, SPY, IVV) have 30% withholding on dividends for Thai residents. Ireland-domiciled versions (CSPX, VUAA) generally get 15%, and accumulating share classes reinvest dividends automatically.
- **Thai S&P 500 feeder funds** are easy to DCA in small amounts and some qualify for tax-advantaged accounts. Check the total fees (feeder fee plus the underlying fund's fee) and whether the fund hedges currency.
- **FX risk:** your returns in THB depend on USD/THB as well as on the index. Over long periods this tends to even out, but it adds volatility.
- **Keep costs low.** A small difference in fees compounds into a big difference over 20 years.

**A simple setup**

1. Choose a fixed amount you can sustain for years, even in a bad month.
2. Automate it on a fixed day each month.
3. Don't skip buys because of the headlines. Investors who keep buying through bear markets tend to do best.
4. Check your plan once or twice a year, not every day.

Bottom line: whether now is a good time depends much more on your situation (horizon, emergency fund, debt, temperament) than on where the market is. If those are in order, starting now is fine, and starting is what matters most.

*This is general information, not personalized financial advice.*
