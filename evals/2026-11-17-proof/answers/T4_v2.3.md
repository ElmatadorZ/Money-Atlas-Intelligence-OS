**Short answer: not on this evidence alone.** A 3-year 25% run tells you which fund did well, not which fund will do well. Before you switch, work out what that 25% came from and what the 2% fee will cost you.

📍 **SITUATION MAP**
- **Given:** Fund A made 25% a year over 3 years and charges 2%. Your bank's fund made 7%.
- **Not known:** whether the 25% is before or after the fee, what each fund holds (are they even the same kind of fund?), the bank fund's fee, any entry or exit charges, tax on selling, and your time horizon.
- **Premise check:** 25% a year for 3 years means the money nearly doubled (1.25³ ≈ 1.95×). The 7% fund grew about 1.23×. A gap that big almost always means the two funds hold different things (for example, a concentrated growth or tech fund against a balanced or bond-heavy one). That's a different level of risk, not a better version of the same product.

📍 **FIRST PRINCIPLE BREAKDOWN**
A fund's return comes from three things: the market it sits in, the manager's skill above that market, and the fee taken off the top.
- **The market:** if Fund A's benchmark index also made about 25% over those years, the manager added little. You could get the same exposure through an index fund for a much lower fee.
- **Skill:** three years is too short to separate skill from luck. Long-running studies such as S&P's SPIVA reports find that most active funds trail their benchmark over long periods, and that top performers rarely stay on top.
- **The fee:** you pay it in every year, good or bad. The return, unlike the fee, isn't guaranteed.

**Fee drag, illustrated** ($10,000 over 20 years, both funds earning the same 8% a year before fees `[ASSUMED]`, bank fund fee 1% `[ASSUMED]`):
- At a 2% fee: 10,000 × 1.06²⁰ ≈ **$32,100**
- At a 1% fee: 10,000 × 1.07²⁰ ≈ **$38,700**
- The extra 1% fee costs about **$6,600**, around 17% of the final balance. Fund A has to beat the bank fund by more than the fee gap every year just to break even.

📍 **SCENARIOS**
- **Skill persists (less likely on base rates):** Fund A keeps beating its own benchmark after fees. *What would confirm it:* it beat its benchmark over 5–10 years, through at least one down market, with the same manager still running it.
- **Mean reversion (more common):** the 25% came from one hot sector or style. When that cools, Fund A falls harder than your bank fund, and you will have bought in after the gains. *What would confirm it:* the benchmark or sector also made about 25%, the holdings are concentrated, and it had a big drawdown in earlier years.
- **Base case:** the two funds take different amounts of risk. The real choice is how much stock or growth exposure you want, not "25% vs 7%."

📍 **DECISION FRAMEWORK**
- **IF** you need this money within about 5 years → a recent high flier with a 2% fee is the wrong tool. Volatility matters more than past returns.
- **IF** your horizon is 10+ years and you want more growth → compare Fund A with a **low-cost index fund in the same category**, not with your bank fund. Pick on fee and fit, not on the last 3 years.
- **IF** you still like Fund A → move part of the money, not all of it, and only after checking exit fees and the tax on selling your current fund.
- **What to check first:** Fund A's factsheet (benchmark, 5- and 10-year returns against the benchmark, worst year, full annual cost and any sales charges), and the same figures for your bank fund. Fees and products change, so get current numbers from the fund house's factsheet or prospectus.

📍 **RISK & FAILURE MODE**
The main risk is buying near the top of a run, then selling after the drawdown and locking in the loss. **This view is wrong if** Fund A has beaten its benchmark after fees over 10+ years under the same manager. Then the fee may be worth paying, at a size you could live with if it fell 30–40% `[ASSUMED, a typical equity bear-market range]`.

**CONFIDENCE: MEDIUM.** It's firm on the fee arithmetic and the general evidence that past winners rarely stay on top. It's limited because I don't know what either fund holds, its fees after costs, or your horizon. **KEY UNKNOWNS:** both funds' benchmarks and holdings, whether the 25% is after fees, switching costs and tax, your time horizon and emergency fund.
