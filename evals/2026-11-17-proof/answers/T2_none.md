**Short answer: No. With these numbers, 2x leverage probably won't get you to 15%. It would give you roughly 10–11% a year, and your drawdowns would be about twice as deep, likely -40% to -60% in a bad crisis.**

## Why 2x doesn't double your return

Leverage doubles your return *and* your borrowing cost. It also raises volatility, which pulls down compound growth. Here's the rough math:

| | Unlevered 60/40 | 2x levered |
|---|---|---|
| Expected arithmetic return | ~8.5% | 2 × 8.5% − 1 × borrow cost |
| Borrow cost (assume ~5%, roughly cash + spread) | — | −5% |
| Arithmetic return | ~8.5% | ~12% |
| Volatility | 10% | 20% |
| Volatility drag (≈ σ²/2) | −0.5% | −2% |
| **Compound (CAGR) return** | **~8%** | **~10%** |

The key number is your **excess return over the borrowing rate**: about 8.5% − 5% = 3.5%. Each extra unit of leverage adds only that spread, while the volatility drag grows with the square of leverage. With these inputs, the best possible compound return is about **11% at ~3.5x leverage**. That's mathematically the most you can get, and nobody should hold anywhere near that much leverage. **No amount of leverage gets this portfolio to 15% a year** unless you borrow much more cheaply or expect much higher returns.

Two more points:
- **Your 8% probably includes a tailwind.** Over the last 40 years, bond yields fell from the teens to near zero, which boosted both bonds and stocks. Starting yields are different now, so 8% going forward is optimistic. A lower base return makes leverage look worse.
- **Leveraging bonds is especially sensitive to borrowing costs.** When the cash rate is near your bond yield, the levered bond portion earns almost nothing extra.

## What drawdown to expect

- **Unlevered 60/40 history:** about −20% to −30% in typical bad periods (2008: about −30%; 2022: about −20% as stocks and bonds fell together). The 1929–32 crash was far worse, roughly −50% or more.
- **At 2x:** roughly **−40% to −60%** in a 2008- or 2022-type event. In 2022, rising rates also pushed up your borrowing cost at the same time, so it would have hurt more.
- **A 1930s-style event would wipe out a 2x portfolio.** A 50% fall at 2x leverage takes your equity to zero.
- **Recovery takes much longer.** After a −50% loss you need +100% to get back to even, and a levered portfolio gets there more slowly.
- **Path risk:** with margin, a sharp drop can trigger **margin calls or forced selling at the bottom**. That turns a temporary loss into a permanent one. Many leveraged investors get hurt this way even when their long-run thesis was right.

## If you still want more return

1. **Check whether you need 15% at all.** Work backwards from your goal: maybe a longer horizon, higher savings rate, or lower spending target gets you there without the risk.
2. **Shift the mix before adding leverage.** 80/20 or 100% equities gives you higher expected return and higher volatility, but no borrowing cost and no margin-call risk. Expect about 9–10%, not 15%.
3. **If you use leverage, keep it modest and structured.** For example 1.2–1.5x, using cheap financing that has no margin calls (futures, box spreads, or leveraged funds that rebalance daily, though those have their own decay). Size it so a −50% equity crash doesn't force you to sell.
4. **Be wary of any plan that "needs" 15% a year.** Very few strategies sustain that, and the ones that try usually come with drawdowns most people can't hold through.

**Bottom line:** 2x leverage probably gives you about 10% a year, not 15%, with double the volatility and a real chance of a −50% or worse drawdown or a margin-call wipeout. You'd be taking on a lot of extra risk for a small amount of extra return.

*These are illustrative estimates, not personal financial advice. Your borrowing rate, taxes, and time horizon change the numbers a lot, so consider running them with a fee-only advisor.*
