**Short answer:** almost certainly yes, move most of it out of the 0.5% account. Which one to use depends on when you might need the money, not on which product "pays more". A sensible default is to split it: a money market fund for money you might need at short notice, and a fixed deposit for money you know you won't touch for the whole term.

Every 1 percentage point on 300,000 baht is worth **3,000 baht a year**. With these example rates, either option earns you about **2,100–2,300 baht a year more** than you get now. Once tax is taken off, the two end up close, so access and safety matter more than the headline rate. If the fixed-deposit rate is at least ~0.3–0.4 points above the fund's yield, it comes out ahead after tax. If not, the fund wins.

### How they actually differ

- **Access:** A money market fund can usually be sold and paid out in about 1 business day. If you break a fixed deposit early, you usually lose some or all of the interest.
- **Rate path:** A fixed deposit locks in today's rate. A money market fund's yield follows short-term rates up or down.
- **Safety:** Bank deposits are covered by Thailand's deposit protection scheme up to 1 million baht per person per bank (as of 2025). A money market fund is **not** covered. It is low-risk because it holds short-term government and bank paper, but its value isn't guaranteed. Check the fund's factsheet for what it holds and its fees, because fees come straight out of the yield.

### Two scenarios to think about

- **Rates keep falling:** A fixed deposit locked in now wins, because the fund's yield will fall with rates. You'd see this in further policy-rate cuts by the Bank of Thailand.
- **Rates hold or rise, or you need the cash early:** The money market fund wins, because it adjusts and you never pay a penalty to get out.

You can't know ahead of time which one will happen, so splitting the money is the robust choice.

### A practical split (adjust to your situation)

1. **Emergency fund (3–6 months of expenses)** goes into a money market fund or a high-interest digital savings account. Some banks pay well above 0.5% on balances up to a limit, so compare.
2. **The rest, if you won't need it for 12+ months,** goes into fixed deposits. You could ladder them (for example, part at 6 months and part at 12 months) so some money matures regularly.
3. **Money for long-term goals (5+ years)** probably shouldn't sit in either one. That's a separate conversation about investing.

### What would change this advice

- When you might need the money (the most important factor)
- Whether you already have an emergency fund elsewhere
- Whether you have any debt costing more than these rates. Paying it off beats any deposit.
- The actual current rates at your bank and the fund you're considering

**Main risk:** You lock everything in a fixed deposit, then need the cash and lose the interest by breaking it early. That's why the emergency portion should stay liquid.
