**Short answer:** You can't decide on these numbers alone. "25% vs 7%" feels like an easy call, but at the moment you're comparing two numbers that may not measure the same thing. Below is how to check it properly. The final decision is yours.

---

📍 **SITUATION MAP**

What you know: Fund A shows "25% annual over 3 years" and charges a 2% fee. Your bank's fund returned 7%.
What you don't know (and it matters more): what each fund holds, how risky each one is, whether the figures are before or after fees, and what fee the bank fund charges.
I don't have live data on either fund, so I won't make up any figures for them.

📍 **FIRST PRINCIPLE BREAKDOWN**

1. **Check that the numbers are comparable.**
   - "25% over 3 years" could mean 25% **per year** (about +95% in total, since 1.25³ ≈ 1.95). It could also mean 25% **in total** over 3 years, which is only about **7.7% per year**. In that second case it is barely ahead of your bank's 7%. Ask which one it is.
   - Is the 25% **net of the 2% fee** or before it? Marketing material often shows the more flattering figure.
   - Is the bank's 7% from the **same period and the same kind of asset**? A fund that's 100% in tech or a single country's stocks shouldn't be compared with a mixed or bond fund. They carry very different risk.

2. **Where the return came from.** A high 3-year return usually comes from being in the sector that happened to do well during those 3 years. That doesn't necessarily mean the manager is skilled. Past performance is a weak predictor of future returns. Many top-ranked funds fall back toward average once their sector's cycle turns.

3. **The fee is the one thing that's certain.** Returns go up and down, but you pay the 2% every year in any market. Example: 100,000 growing at 7% a year for 20 years becomes about **387,000**. At 5% (the same return minus 2% in fees) it becomes about **265,000**. A 2% fee can cost you roughly 30% of your final wealth over the long run.

📍 **SYSTEM MAP**

Macro, interest rates and liquidity → the sector Fund A invests in → that sector's 3-year return → the "25%" in the advert.
If the conditions that pushed that sector up (cheap money, a strong theme, fund inflows) change direction, the 25% can turn negative quickly. You would still be paying the 2%.

📍 **SMC LAYER MAP (applied to funds)**

A fund that's being heavily marketed off a strong 3-year record often sits around **L3 (Decision Zone) to L4 (Distribution)** of the cycle. That's when ordinary investors move in after most of the gain has already happened.
This is a **framework view, not a measurement**. To place it properly I'd need the fund's holdings and its recent drawdown.
**Invalidation:** this reading is wrong if the fund has beaten its benchmark consistently for more than 10 years and through at least one down market.

📍 **NARRATIVE INTELLIGENCE**

The story you're being told: "This fund makes 25%, your bank's fund makes 7%, so you're losing out by staying."
What's usually true: the fund is advertised **because** its recent results are good (survivorship and selection bias). The funds that crashed don't get advertised.

📍 **SCENARIOS**

🐂 **Bull:** The fund's strategy is a real edge and keeps beating its benchmark after fees. Switching pays off.
   *Condition:* a long record (10+ years) of beating its benchmark, including through bad years.
🐻 **Bear:** The sector cycle turns. The fund drops 30–50% from its peak (a common size of drop for concentrated equity funds, not a forecast for this fund) and you still pay 2% a year. You end up behind the bank fund.
   *Trigger:* you buy after the strong run, close to the peak.
⚖️ **Base (most likely):** Over the long run the fund's return after fees ends up close to its benchmark or below it. The real difference is mainly higher risk plus higher fees.

📍 **DECISION FRAMEWORK**

- **First, ask for 5 things:** (1) is the 25% per year or cumulative, (2) is it net of fees, (3) the 5–10-year record compared with the benchmark, (4) the worst drop (max drawdown), (5) the bank fund's fee and what it holds.
- **IF** you'll need the money within 1–3 years → a high-volatility fund isn't a good fit. Even if the long-term return is good, you could have to sell during a drop.
- **IF** your horizon is 10+ years and you can watch it fall 30–50% without selling → you can consider a higher-risk fund. Even then, compare it with a **low-cost index fund** in the same asset class. That often beats both a 2% fund and the bank fund.
- **IF** you want to try it → don't move everything at once. Try a portion (for example 10–20%) or buy in gradually (DCA). Also check for exit and entry fees and any tax effects of switching.
- **IF** the 25% turns out to be cumulative over 3 years (about 7.7% a year) → paying a 2% fee for that is very hard to justify.

📍 **RISK & FAILURE MODE**

- This analysis is wrong if Fund A really is a low-risk fund that has beaten the market after fees for 10+ years. That's possible, but it's rare.
- `[UNVERIFIED]` I don't know the real risk, holdings or fees of either fund. Everything above is a framework, not a verdict on these specific funds.
- The biggest risk isn't picking the "wrong" fund. It's chasing past performance and then selling in a panic when it drops.

**CONFIDENCE:** 70% that the right move is "don't switch yet. Verify the 5 things first." 0% confidence about which fund will give the higher return. Without data, nobody can know that.
**KEY UNKNOWNS:** whether the 25% is annual or cumulative, net or gross of fees, the asset type and risk of each fund, the bank fund's fee, your time horizon, and how much loss you can tolerate.
