Not on those numbers alone. "25% vs 7%" looks like an easy choice, but it tells you less than it seems to. Here's how to work it out properly.

## 1. Three good years don't predict the next three
A 25% annual return over 3 years usually means one of three things:
- **It took a lot more risk.** Concentrated sectors (tech, AI, small caps, crypto, one country) or leverage can produce 25% in a good market and lose 30-40% in a bad one.
- **It caught a good period.** Many funds look like geniuses for 3 years and then fall back to average or worse. Studies keep finding that top-performing funds rarely stay on top.
- **It's not real.** Steady, high returns with little volatility are a classic sign of fraud or misleading reporting. Check that the fund is registered with your regulator (in Thailand, the SEC Thailand database).

The bank fund's 7% may come from a much safer mix, such as bonds or a balanced portfolio. If so, you're comparing two different things.

## 2. Compare like with like
Ask these about both funds:
- **What does it hold?** Equity, bonds, mixed, a single sector, foreign markets?
- **What's its benchmark, and did it beat it?** If the 25% fund's benchmark index returned 28%, it actually *underperformed* and charged you 2% for that.
- **Worst year and biggest drawdown:** how much did it fall at its lowest point? Could you hold on through that without selling?
- **Is the 25% before or after fees?** Get the net figure.
- **Longer track record:** 5-10 years, including 2020 and 2022 if it existed then.

## 3. The 2% fee costs more than it looks
Fees are the one part of returns that's guaranteed. Over long periods they compound against you:

| Starting with 1,000,000, 7% gross return, 20 years | End value |
|---|---|
| Fee 0.5% (net 6.5%) | ~3.52 million |
| Fee 2.0% (net 5.0%) | ~2.65 million |

That's roughly **870,000 lost to fees**, about 25% of the outcome. A 2% fee only pays off if the fund *keeps* beating cheaper alternatives by more than 2% a year, and most active funds don't.

Also find out your bank fund's fee. It might be high too, which would be a reason to look at low-cost index funds rather than either of these two.

## 4. Switching costs
- Selling may trigger front-end/back-end fees, or tax.
- Leaving a tax-advantaged fund (such as SSF/RMF in Thailand) early can mean losing the tax benefit or paying it back.
- Moving right after a strong run often means buying near the top.

## A practical way forward
1. Get both funds' fact sheets and compare holdings, benchmark, 5-10 year returns, worst drawdown, and total fees.
2. Decide what the money is for and when you need it. Money needed within 3-5 years shouldn't be in a high-volatility fund.
3. If you still like the high-return fund, **don't move everything**. Put a portion in (for example 10-20%) that you could watch fall 30% without panicking.
4. Seriously consider a low-cost index fund as a third option. Over the long run it often beats both.

**Bottom line:** a higher past return isn't enough reason to switch. Switch only if the new fund fits your goals and risk tolerance, has a long record of beating its own benchmark *after* its 2% fee, and is legitimately registered. If you're moving a large amount, a fee-only financial advisor (one who doesn't earn commission on what you buy) is worth paying for.
