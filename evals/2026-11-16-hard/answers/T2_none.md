**Short answer: No. 2x leverage on this portfolio will probably get you about 9–10% a year, not 15%, and you should plan for drawdowns of 40–60%.** At this portfolio's risk/return profile, no amount of leverage reliably gets you to 15%.

## Why 2x doesn't double your return

Leverage doubles your return above the borrowing cost, not your whole return. It also doubles volatility, and higher volatility pulls down compound growth.

Assumptions: your 8% is a compound (CAGR) figure, and you can borrow at about 5% (cash rate plus a broker spread; retail margin is often higher).

| | 1x (now) | 2x levered |
|---|---|---|
| Average (arithmetic) return | ~8.5% | 2 × 8.5% − 5% borrowing ≈ **12%** |
| Volatility | 10% | **20%** |
| Volatility drag (≈ σ²/2) | −0.5% | **−2%** |
| **Expected compound return** | **~8%** | **~10%** (minus fees and rebalancing costs) |

To get 15% at 2x, you'd need to borrow at roughly 0%.

## Even more leverage doesn't reach 15%

Expected compound return at leverage L is roughly: 5% + L × 3.5% − L² × 0.5%.

- It peaks at about **3.5x, where you'd get only ~11%**, with 35% volatility.
- Going past that point lowers your expected compound return.
- Even if you could borrow at 4%, the best case is about 14% at 4.5x leverage, which is a wildly risky position.

The real limit is the portfolio's risk-adjusted return (Sharpe ratio ≈ (8 − 5)/10 = 0.3). Leverage can't improve that ratio.

## Drawdowns to expect at 2x

- **Historical 60/40 drawdowns:** about −30% to −35% in 2008, about −20% in 2022, and severe real (after-inflation) losses in the 1970s.
- **At 2x:** roughly **−40% in a 2022-type year** and **−55% to −65% in a 2008-type crisis**. Borrowing costs and selling at the bottom (margin calls or forced deleveraging) make it worse.
- **Recovery is slow:** a −50% loss needs a +100% gain to get back to even. At ~10% a year, that's about 7 years.
- **2022 is the worst case for this setup:** stocks and bonds fell together while borrowing costs jumped. That's exactly when leverage on a 60/40 hurts most.

Also, the 8%/10% numbers likely come from a strong period for bonds (falling rates from 1982 to 2020). Forward-looking estimates for 60/40 are often lower, around 5–7%, which would make the leveraged outcome worse.

## What to do instead

1. **Check whether you need 15%.** Work backward from your goal. Saving more or giving yourself more time is often cheaper than taking more risk.
2. **If you want more return, cut bonds before borrowing.** For example, 80/20 or 100% global equities historically gave about 9–10% with 15–17% volatility, with no borrowing cost and no margin-call risk. Expect drawdowns of 40–50%.
3. **If you still want leverage**, keep it modest (1.2–1.5x) and use cheap, non-recallable financing such as futures or a capital-efficient fund, not retail margin. Rebalance on a fixed schedule. Only do it with money you won't need for 10+ years.
4. **Stress-test your behaviour.** Ask yourself honestly whether you'd hold through a −50% loss. If not, the realistic return is far below 10%, because selling at the bottom locks in the loss.

**Bottom line:** at 2x, expect about 9–10% a year with 20% volatility and possible 50%+ drawdowns. The 15% target isn't realistic with this portfolio, even with leverage.

*This is general education, not personalised financial advice. Your borrowing rate, taxes, and time horizon change the numbers.*
