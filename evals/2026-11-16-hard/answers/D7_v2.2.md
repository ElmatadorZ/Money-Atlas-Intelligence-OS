**The fair 1-year forward is about 35.80, and no, it does not mean the baht will weaken.** The forward is *below* spot, so it prices a *stronger* baht. Even that is not a forecast. It is the result of an arbitrage rule.

**The calculation (covered interest parity)**

USDTHB is quoted as baht per dollar, so the baht rate goes on top and the dollar rate goes underneath:

F = Spot × (1 + r_THB) / (1 + r_USD)
F = 36.50 × 1.02 / 1.04 = 37.23 / 1.04 ≈ **35.80**

That is about −0.70 baht in forward points (roughly −1.9%). The dollar trades at a forward discount and the baht at a forward premium.

**Why it has to be this number**

There are two ways to turn 36.50 baht into dollars a year from now. Both have to give the same result, or someone could lock in a riskless profit:

- Deposit the baht at 2%, which gives 37.23 baht. Then convert it at the forward rate F.
- Convert to $1 today and deposit it at 4%, which gives $1.04.

For the two to match, 37.23 / F has to equal 1.04, so F = 35.80. The higher-yielding currency (the USD here) trades at a forward discount by about the size of the rate gap. If it didn't, you would get the extra 2% of interest for free with no currency risk.

**Why it doesn't predict the spot rate**

- **The forward is not an expectation.** It comes from the two interest rates, not from what anyone thinks will happen to USDTHB. Say a forecaster expects 38.00 a year out. The fair forward is still 35.80, because that is what the two deposit rates force.
- **The "it must weaken" idea is a different theory.** It comes from uncovered interest parity: the low-yield currency should *appreciate* enough to cancel the rate gap, which would mean a stronger baht. In practice, that theory fails often over horizons up to a few years. High-yield currencies have tended not to depreciate as much as the rate gap implies, and that failure is why carry trades exist. So the forward is a poor predictor in both directions.
- **What actually moves the baht a year from now** is outside this formula: changes to Fed and BOT policy, the broad dollar, Thailand's current account (tourism, exports, gold flows), and portfolio flows. In one path the Fed cuts faster than the BOT and the dollar weakens broadly, and spot could finish below 35.80. In another path, Thai growth disappoints, the BOT cuts, or there is a risk-off dollar rally, and spot could finish well above 36.50. You can't tell which is coming from the forward. You can only tell by watching those drivers.

**Caveats on the number**

The 35.80 assumes simple annual compounding and that both rates are for the same 1-year term and the same credit quality. On a real dealer quote, you would also see:

- day-count differences (THB uses ACT/365, USD uses ACT/360)
- the bid/ask spread
- the cross-currency basis, which is where offshore and onshore THB pricing can differ

These change the forward points a little, not the direction.

**Why it matters:** A Thai importer who has to pay dollars in a year can lock in about 35.80 today. That is cheaper than spot, because they keep earning the 2% baht rate in the meantime instead of the 4% dollar rate. The rate is cheaper because of the interest gap, not because the market expects the baht to rise.
