**FULL MODE: "Indicator called 9 of the last 10 tops. Sell everything next time?"**

Short answer: "9 of 10 tops" doesn't tell you enough to bet the whole portfolio on one signal. That number only counts the times the indicator was right. It leaves out the times it was wrong. I can't judge this indicator yet because I don't know which one it is, which market it's on, or what its full signal history looks like. What I can do is show you how to test it, and how to act on it without staking everything on one outcome.

📍 **SITUATION MAP**
- Inputs I have: one claim ("called 9 of the last 10 market tops").
- Inputs I don't have: the indicator's name and rules, the asset and timeframe, the total number of times it fired, how long before each top it fired, and how far price rose before the top. I have no current price or market data, so I won't guess where any market is now.

📍 **FIRST PRINCIPLE BREAKDOWN**
The question that matters is "when it fires, how often is a top actually close?" That's a different question from "how many tops did it catch?"

1. **Hit rate vs. precision.** "Caught 9 of 10 tops" measures recall. It says nothing about false alarms. Suppose it fired 40 times and only 9 of those were near a real top. Then each signal is right less than a quarter of the time. (40 is a made-up number to show the math, not data.) An indicator that fires constantly will "catch every top" and still be useless.
2. **Timing.** Did it fire on the day of the top, or 6 to 18 months early? A signal that fires early and is "right eventually" can still mean selling before a large final rally.
3. **Definition of "top."** Who decided what counted as one of the 10 tops, and when? If the tops were picked after the fact, or the indicator's settings were tuned on those same 10 events, the 9/10 is fitted to past data (curve-fitting), not a proven edge.
4. **Sample size.** Ten events is very little. 9/10 is consistent with a real edge, but also with luck and selective framing. You can't tell the two apart with n=10.
5. **Who's telling you.** "9 of 10 tops" is a common marketing line for indicators. Ask whether the person quoting it shows the full signal log or only the wins.

📍 **SYSTEM MAP**
Macro and liquidity regime → how tops form → whether the indicator's logic still works.
Most top indicators measure some form of excess: momentum stretch, sentiment, valuation, or leverage. They work when tops come from excess building up. They fail when the regime changes, for example when liquidity keeps expanding and "overbought" stays overbought for a long time, or when a top comes from an outside shock the indicator can't see. An indicator tuned in one regime can quietly stop working in the next.

📍 **SMC LAYER MAP**
I can't place any market in L1–L5 without price structure and an asset. Here is the framework only. A top signal is most useful when it agrees with structure, meaning L4 (Distribution: fake breakouts, weakening follow-through) or L5 (Exit Liquidity). A signal that fires while structure is still in L2 (Expansion) is the classic early-exit trap.

📍 **NARRATIVE INTELLIGENCE**
The popular story says "this indicator predicts tops, so obey it." What usually happens: the misses are forgotten, the false signals are left out, and the one big hit gets repeated. Treat a clean track record as something to check before you trust it.

📍 **SCENARIOS (for the next time it fires)**
🐂 **Bull (false signal):** It fires and price keeps rising. Condition: structure still trending, no distribution behaviour, liquidity expanding. If you sold everything, you miss the rally, may pay tax on the sale, and face the hard choice of when to buy back.
🐻 **Bear (real top):** It fires and a major decline follows. Condition: the signal agrees with distribution structure, weakening breadth or momentum, and a worsening macro or liquidity backdrop. Selling protects capital.
⚖️ **Base (most probable):** It fires early. Price rises somewhat further, chops, and then a top forms some time later. A partial, rules-based reduction works better here than all-or-nothing, in either direction.

📍 **DECISION FRAMEWORK**
First, audit the indicator. These are the inputs I'd need to give you a confident read:
- Total number of signals, not just the hits. Calculate the share of signals that led to a meaningful decline within a set window.
- Average lead time and how far price went up between the signal and the actual top.
- Whether it was tested on data it wasn't designed or tuned on (out-of-sample).
- How a simple "sell on signal, buy back on X" rule would have done against just holding, after costs and taxes.

Then size the action to the evidence:
- **IF your timeframe is long-term (years):** one signal is weak grounds for a full exit. Consider trimming a portion or rebalancing back to your target allocation instead.
- **IF your timeframe is short or tactical:** use the signal as a trigger to tighten risk (smaller position, defined stop, hedge). Wait for structure to confirm before acting more.
- **IF you have low risk tolerance or need the money soon:** that's a reason to lower risk now, through your allocation, rather than waiting on any indicator.
- **IF you go ahead anyway:** write down in advance how and when you would buy back. Selling is only half of the decision.

The decision is yours. This is a way to think it through, not personal investment advice.

📍 **RISK & FAILURE MODE**
- This whole analysis would change if the full signal log showed high precision (most signals followed by a real top within a short window) and that held up on out-of-sample data. In that case the indicator would deserve more weight, but still not "everything".
- My view would be wrong if the indicator really does have strong, repeatable precision and short lead times. I have no data to judge either way.
- The biggest risk of going all-in on one signal is concentration of the decision itself. One input then controls your whole portfolio, and you'll still need a second correct call to get back in.
