# If the Strait of Hormuz closes for two weeks: oil, gold, the dollar and Thai stocks

**Short answer:** Oil would spike hard. Gold would get a safe-haven bid. The US dollar would gain against most currencies, especially against oil importers like Thailand. Thai stocks would fall overall, though energy and refining names would hold up much better than the rest. Two weeks is short, so the key question is whether markets believe it will really end in two weeks. If they believe it, much of the move reverses once ships start sailing again.

*Note: I don't have live prices here, so the moves below are rough percentage ranges, not price targets.*

---

## Why Hormuz matters

- About **20% of the world's oil** (roughly 17–20 million barrels a day of crude and products) and **about 20% of global LNG** (mostly from Qatar) pass through the strait.
- Bypass routes are limited. Saudi Arabia's East-West pipeline to the Red Sea and the UAE's Habshan–Fujairah pipeline together might carry an extra 3–6 mb/d. Iraq, Kuwait, Qatar and Bahrain have almost no way around it.
- **Net loss: roughly 10–15 mb/d for two weeks.** No supply shock in modern history comes close. The 1973 embargo and the 1990 invasion of Kuwait each removed about 4–5 mb/d.
- **Cushions:** IEA strategic reserves (a coordinated release is likely within days), commercial inventories, and oil already on tankers outside the Gulf. Two weeks of disruption is roughly 150–200 million barrels, which reserves can partly cover. That is why duration matters so much.

---

## 1. Oil: the epicenter

| Phase | What likely happens |
|---|---|
| Days 1–3 | Brent jumps sharply, plausibly **+20% to +50%** depending on the starting price and how open-ended the closure looks. Intraday swings are extreme. |
| During closure | Strong **backwardation** (near-month contracts much higher than later months). Tanker freight rates and war-risk insurance surge. Diesel and jet fuel cracks rise even more than crude. Asian buyers scramble for Atlantic Basin (US, Brazil, West Africa) cargoes. |
| After reopening | A fast but partial retracement. A risk premium stays in for weeks. Delivery delays and reserve refilling keep physical markets tight for 1–2 months. |

**The key distinction:** if markets think it's "two weeks and done", the spike is sharp but short-lived. If they fear it could drag on, prices could overshoot well beyond the ranges above. LNG would be hit as hard or harder, since there is little spare capacity to replace Qatari supply. Asian spot LNG prices would jump.

---

## 2. Gold: up, but less than you might expect

- **Supports:** safe-haven demand, geopolitical fear, and inflation worries from expensive energy. Central-bank buying stays steady in the background.
- **Headwinds:** a stronger dollar and higher bond yields (from an inflation scare) can cap the rally. Gold can also dip briefly if leveraged investors sell it to cover losses elsewhere, as in the first days of past panics.
- **Likely path:** a rise of a few percent, maybe **+3% to +8%** at the peak of the fear. Some of that gets given back after reopening. But if the episode pushes central banks to stay cautious or deepens de-dollarization worries, gold can keep a higher floor afterwards.

---

## 3. US dollar: a relative winner

- **Safe-haven demand:** money flows into USD and US Treasuries. Note that the yen and Swiss franc are less reliable havens in an oil shock because Japan is a big energy importer.
- **Terms of trade:** the US is now a **net petroleum exporter**, so expensive oil helps the US relative to big importers like the eurozone, Japan, India, Korea and Thailand.
- **The Fed:** an oil spike lifts inflation expectations, which makes it harder for the Fed to cut rates. That also supports the dollar.
- **Biggest losers among currencies:** Asian importers. That includes THB, KRW, INR and PHP, plus JPY.
- **Winners against the trend:** oil-exporter currencies like NOK, CAD and possibly MXN, although broad risk-off selling can mute that.

---

## 4. Thai stocks (SET): the overall index falls, energy holds up

Thailand is highly exposed. It imports most of its crude, much of it from the Middle East, and it relies on imported LNG for power generation.

**Macro effects:**
- **THB weakens** as the import bill rises and foreign investors pull money out of emerging markets.
- **Inflation and costs rise.** The government would likely use the **Oil Fuel Fund** to cap diesel prices, which strains public finances. Power tariffs (the Ft charge) face upward pressure from LNG costs.
- **Foreign selling** is likely to hit large-cap stocks across the board in the first days.
- **Likely SET move:** a drop of several percent at the worst point, roughly **-5% to -10%**, with high volatility. The rebound depends on how quickly the strait reopens.

**By sector:**

| Likely to outperform | Likely to underperform |
|---|---|
| **Upstream E&P (PTTEP):** a direct beneficiary of higher oil and gas prices | **Airlines (AAV, THAI, etc.):** jet fuel is their biggest cost, and tourism takes a hit from fear |
| **Refiners (TOP, BCP, SPRC, IRPC):** wider crack spreads and inventory gains, though crude supply security and government price pressure are risks | **Petrochemicals (e.g. PTTGC, IVL):** feedstock costs jump while demand weakens |
| **PTT:** benefits overall, but its gas business and policy pressure limit the upside | **Transport and logistics, consumer, retail:** margin squeeze and weaker purchasing power |
| | **Gas-fired power producers:** depends on whether fuel costs can be passed through, and with what lag |
| | **Tourism and hotels:** risk-off sentiment, costlier flights, and possible Middle East air-route disruption |

---

## Summary

| Asset | During the 2-week closure | After reopening |
|---|---|---|
| Oil (Brent) | Sharp spike, strong backwardation | Fast but partial pullback, risk premium remains |
| LNG (Asia) | Spikes, possibly more than oil | Normalizes slowly |
| Gold | Up moderately | Gives back some gains, may keep a higher floor |
| USD | Stronger, especially vs. Asian importers | Eases gradually |
| THB | Weaker | Recovers along with oil |
| SET | Falls, energy holds up relatively well | Rebounds, led by airlines, tourism and consumer stocks |

## What to watch
1. **Credibility of "two weeks":** official statements, naval escorts, and whether tankers actually start moving again.
2. **Size and speed of any IEA strategic reserve release.**
3. **Brent's time spreads:** extreme backwardation signals real physical shortage, not just fear.
4. **USD/THB and foreign net flows into the SET.**
5. **Thai government response:** Oil Fuel Fund, diesel caps and Ft tariff decisions.

**Key lesson from history:** markets usually overreact to the headline and then fade quickly once supply flows again. The big damage comes only if a "short" disruption turns into a long one. Size positions around that uncertainty rather than around a single forecast.

*This is scenario analysis for educational purposes, not investment advice.*
