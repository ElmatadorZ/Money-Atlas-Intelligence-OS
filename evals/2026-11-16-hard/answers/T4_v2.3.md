- **What you gave me:** Fund A returned 25%/yr over 3 years and charges a 2% fee. Your bank's fund returned 7% (period not stated).

**Premise check:** 25% a year for 3 years means the money roughly doubled (1.25³ ≈ 1.95×). That's possible, but it usually means the fund sat in whatever sector or theme ran hardest in that window. That is not the same as skill. Three years is too short to tell skill from luck or from a hot market.

📍 **FIRST PRINCIPLE BREAKDOWN**

The return you get = what the underlying assets earn − fees − your switching costs.

- **The fee is certain. The return isn't.** Funds that top recent rankings tend to drift back toward their asset class average. Chasing last period's winner is one of the most common ways people buy near the top.
- **Fee drag, worked out.** Say Fund A's *future* gross return is 8%/yr `[ASSUMED: a broad equity-like long-run figure, not a forecast]`. After the 2% fee you get 6%. On 100,000 over 10 years:
  - at 6% net → ~179,100
  - at 7% net (your bank fund, if 7% is after its fees) → ~196,700
  - **The fee alone costs about 37,000 per 100,000 over 10 years** (8% gross = ~215,900 vs 6% net = ~179,100).
- **Break-even:** Fund A only beats a 7% net fund if it keeps earning more than **9% gross**, every year, for as long as you hold it.

**You may be comparing different things.** If the bank fund holds bonds or a mix and Fund A is all stocks or one sector, this isn't "good fund vs bad fund". It's a choice between two risk levels.

---

📍 **SCENARIOS**

- **Skill is real:** Fund A beat *its own benchmark* (not just your bank fund) over 5–10 years, under the same manager, with a strategy you can explain in one sentence. **Trigger to believe it:** the factsheet shows consistent outperformance through at least one down year.
- **Hot market fades (common for recent top performers):** the 25% came mostly from its sector rising, and its benchmark also returned around 20–25%. **Trigger:** concentrated holdings, a short track record, or big inflows after the good run. Then future returns fall back while the 2% fee stays.
- **Base case:** Fund A's future return lands near its asset class average minus 2%, which may be close to or below your bank fund. The gap between them may come down mainly to fees and risk level.

---

📍 **DECISION FRAMEWORK**

Check these four things on both funds' factsheets before deciding:
1. Is 25% **after** fees, and is it **annualised** or cumulative?
2. How did it compare with **its benchmark** over the same 3 years?
3. What's its **worst year or largest drawdown**, and could you hold through that?
4. What's the **total cost of switching** (exit fees, front-end load, taxes)?

- **IF you need the money within ~5 years** → don't chase a high-volatility fund on 3 years of data.
- **IF your horizon is 10+ years and you want more growth** → compare Fund A with a **low-fee index fund** of the same asset class. A 2% fee needs a strong reason to beat that.
- **IF you still want exposure** → move a portion and build up over time (DCA), rather than switching everything at once. How much you move matters more than which fund you pick.
