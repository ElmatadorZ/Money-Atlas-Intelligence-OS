**Short answer: no, not as a way to hit 15%.** Doubling a portfolio that returned 8% does not give you 16%. Once you pay to borrow and account for volatility drag, a realistic expectation is about **10-11% a year**, roughly twice the swings, and a bad-year drawdown that can be large enough to force you out at the bottom. Only with free borrowing does the math reach 15%.

📍 SITUATION MAP
- **Given:** 60/40 portfolio, 8% a year, 10% volatility. Target 15% a year. Proposal: 2x leverage.
- **Premise check:** the 8% is a *past* figure. Most of the 60/40 track record people quote comes from a long period of falling interest rates, which pushed bond prices up, and that tailwind does not repeat on its own. Forward returns could be lower than 8%. If they are, everything below gets worse.
- **Not known here:** your borrowing rate, the leverage method (margin, futures, a leveraged fund), your time horizon, income stability, and whether you could hold through a loss of half the portfolio.

📍 FIRST PRINCIPLE BREAKDOWN — the arithmetic
Assumptions: borrowing cost **5% a year [ASSUMED]**, roughly a broker margin rate in a 4-5% policy-rate world; you should replace it with your actual rate. Returns are treated as roughly normal **[ASSUMED]**, which understates tail risk.

1. Treat 8% as the compound (geometric) return. The average annual return is then ≈ 8% + ½·(10%)² ≈ **8.5%**.
2. At 2x, the average return is 2 × 8.5% − 1 × 5% borrowing = **12%**.
3. Volatility doubles to **20%**, and the drag grows with the square of volatility: ½·(20%)² = **2%**.
4. Expected compound return ≈ 12% − 2% = **~10% a year**.

| Borrowing cost | Expected compound return at 2x |
|---|---|
| 0% | ~15% |
| 3% | ~12% |
| 5% [ASSUMED] | ~10% |
| 7% | ~8% (no better than unlevered, with double the risk) |

Your 15% target only works if borrowing is essentially free. On the base case, it takes about 12% more risk to earn about 2% more return.

📍 DRAWDOWN — what to expect
- **Typical bad year:** with an average of 12% and volatility of 20%, the model gives roughly a **1-in-4 chance of a losing year** (computed: P(return < 0) at z ≈ −0.6). A one-in-twenty bad year is about **−20%**.
- **Crisis year:** in the 2008 crisis an unlevered 60/40 fell by **roughly a third** from peak to trough. At 2x that becomes **roughly 55-65%** before borrowing costs. It is also worse in years like 2022, when stocks and bonds fell together and the 40% in bonds gave no cushion. [Historical sizes are approximate, not exact prints.]
- **The real killer is the margin call.** If you borrow 1:1 on margin and your broker requires 25% maintenance equity, the portfolio only needs to drop about **33%** for a call: (A − 1)/A = 0.25 gives A = 1.33. A 2008-type decline gets there. You are then forced to sell near the low and lock in the loss, and a −60% hole needs +150% just to break even.

📍 SCENARIOS
- 🐂 **Bull:** stocks and bonds both deliver near their history, rates fall, and your borrowing is cheap (≤2%). You get 13-15%. *Trigger:* a falling policy rate with a steady stock-bond hedge.
- 🐻 **Bear:** stocks and bonds fall together or rates stay high (a 2022-type regime). Borrowing eats the edge, a 40-60% drawdown hits, and you face a margin call or capitulate. *Trigger:* stocks and bonds moving in the same direction during sell-offs, and rising rates.
- ⚖️ **Base (most likely):** about 10% compound with a few painful years. Over 10 years, the model puts the chance of averaging 15% or more at about **1 in 5** (computed: annualized standard deviation ≈ 20%/√10 ≈ 6.3%, so 15% sits about 0.8 standard deviations above the 10% expectation).

📍 DECISION FRAMEWORK
- **If you cannot get borrowing well below ~4%** → leverage does not pay for its risk. Don't do it.
- **If a 50%+ paper loss would make you sell** → 2x will most likely give you a *worse* outcome than staying unlevered.
- **If you still want more return** → the cleaner levers are: a higher stock weight without borrowing (no margin-call risk), a higher savings rate, and a longer horizon. If you do use leverage, use a modest amount (e.g. 1.2-1.4x), don't use 1:1 margin, and keep a cash buffer far from the margin line.
- **First, test the target itself.** 15% a year is above what a diversified portfolio has delivered over long periods. Ask whether the goal can be met by changing the time or the contributions instead of the risk.

📍 RISK & FAILURE MODE
The analysis breaks if: your borrowing cost is very different from 5%; the leverage is done through daily-reset leveraged ETFs, which add more drag in choppy markets; or returns have fatter tails than the normal model, which history suggests they do, so real drawdowns are likely *worse* than shown.

CONFIDENCE: **HIGH** on the mechanics (borrowing cost and volatility drag reduce levered returns, and drawdowns scale with leverage, which is arithmetic). **MEDIUM-LOW** on the specific 10% figure, which rests on an assumed 5% borrowing rate and on past 60/40 returns continuing. | KEY UNKNOWNS: your borrowing rate, leverage method, horizon, liquidity needs, loss tolerance.
