**Short answer:** oil spikes, the dollar firms, gold gets a safe-haven bid but less than you'd expect, and Thai stocks fall, except energy names. How big and how long each move is depends far more on whether markets *believe* it is only two weeks than on the two weeks themselves.

📍 SITUATION MAP
- **Given:** a hypothetical full closure lasting two weeks. No prices were supplied, and I have no live feed, so there are no levels below.
- **Last known (structural, as of my training data, mid-2026):** roughly a fifth of world oil consumption and a large share of global LNG trade (mostly from Qatar) passes through Hormuz. The Saudi and UAE bypass pipelines can reroute only a fraction of that. Thailand is a large net importer of crude, and much of it comes from the Gulf.
- **Premise check:** a full closure has never actually happened, even during the 1980s Tanker War. More importantly, nobody would know on day one that it would last "two weeks." Markets price the *fear of an open-ended* closure, and then reprice when ships move again.
- **Unknown:** current prices, how much spare inventory there is today, the Fed's stance, and how much is already priced in.

📍 FIRST PRINCIPLE BREAKDOWN
This is a **physical flow shock**, not a demand shock. For a sense of scale: about 20 mb/d `[ASSUMED, from the "one-fifth" share]` minus bypass capacity, over 14 days, comes to a few hundred million barrels lost. That is painful but finite. IEA emergency stocks and the US SPR exist precisely for a gap of this size. So the damage depends on **duration and credibility**, not on the first headline.

📍 SYSTEM MAP
Closure → **Oil/LNG:** front-month prices jump sharply, the curve goes into steep backwardation, and freight and insurance rates explode. → **Inflation expectations rise** → central banks can't ease, so real yields stay firm. → **USD:** gets a safe-haven bid, and the US as a net petroleum exporter gains on terms of trade against importers in Asia and Europe. → **Gold:** the haven bid runs into a stronger dollar and firm real yields, so it rises but is capped. → **Thailand:** the oil import bill widens, the baht weakens, foreign investors sell EM risk, and the SET falls.

**Thai stocks, by sector:**
- **Hurt:** airlines (fuel, plus possible Gulf airspace and hub disruption), petrochemicals and refiners (feedstock and margin squeeze, mixed), transport, and consumer names (inflation hits spending). Tourism suffers if Gulf transit routes are disrupted.
- **Relative winners:** upstream E&P (e.g., PTTEP) and integrated energy. Because energy is a heavy SET weight, the index may fall *less* than its sectors suggest.
- **Policy buffer:** the Oil Fuel Fund has historically capped retail diesel prices, which delays the hit to consumers but drains the fund. Check its current status with the Energy Policy and Planning Office.

📍 SCENARIOS
- 🐂 **Shock extends (oil bull):** *Trigger:* attacks on Gulf infrastructure, mines, or no clear end date. Oil keeps rising, the dollar strengthens further, gold breaks higher as stagflation fears take over from real-yield logic, and the SET and baht both slide. *Condition:* the "two weeks" becomes open-ended.
- 🐻 **Fast reopening (oil bear after the spike):** *Trigger:* naval escorts, a diplomatic deal, or tankers transiting by week two. The spike fully retraces and the risk premium collapses. Gold gives back its haven bid, the SET recovers and airlines rebound hardest.
- ⚖️ **Base (most likely as stated, a closure that really ends in two weeks):** a sharp but temporary spike, with curve backwardation doing most of the work and long-dated prices moving much less. The dollar ends firmer, gold ends modestly higher, and the SET dips with heavy rotation into energy and out of airlines and consumer names. Most of it reverses within weeks of reopening. *Why most likely:* the inventory buffer covers a finite gap.

📍 DECISION FRAMEWORK
- **If you trade short-term:** the trade is volatility, not direction. Spikes driven by headlines reverse violently on the first "ships moving" news.
- **If you invest long-term:** a two-week shock is usually noise. Panic-selling at the low has historically been the costlier mistake. Check concentration instead: are you heavy in airlines or consumer names?
- **If you hold Thai assets without hedges:** baht weakness hurts unhedged foreign-currency costs. Size positions; don't bet on the outcome.

📍 RISK & FAILURE MODE
This analysis is invalidated if: (1) the closure comes with **physical damage** to production or export terminals, which turns a flow problem into a supply loss; (2) **LNG** becomes the dominant story, since Asian gas prices and Thai power costs could matter more than crude; or (3) a recession scare overwhelms the inflation channel, in which case oil and the dollar fade faster and gold leads.

**CONFIDENCE:** MEDIUM on **direction** (the mechanisms are well established and consistent with past Gulf scares). LOW on **magnitude** and timing. Price levels are not assessable without live data. | **KEY UNKNOWNS:** current prices and positioning, inventory levels, Fed stance, whether infrastructure is damaged, how much is already priced in.
