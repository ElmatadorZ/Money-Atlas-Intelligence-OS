**Short answer:** It's cheaper than it was. Whether it's actually *cheap* depends on whether the "E" in that P/E is going to hold up, and whether 16 is still the right average to compare against.

### What the numbers do tell you
- A P/E of 13 against a 10-year average of 16 is about a 19% discount. If earnings hold and the multiple drifts back toward 16, that's roughly 20%+ upside before dividends. The SET's dividend yield is usually around 3–4%, which helps while you wait.
- A 15% fall in 3 months is a sharp move. It usually means a lot of bad news is already in the price.

### Why "below average" doesn't automatically mean "cheap"
1. **Which earnings?** If the 13x uses trailing earnings and analysts are cutting forecasts, the forward P/E could be closer to 14–15x. A falling market with falling earnings can stay "cheap" for a long time. Check whether forward EPS estimates have been revised down over the same 3 months.
2. **The 10-year average may be the wrong benchmark.** Thailand's market has been de-rating for structural reasons: slow GDP growth (around 2–3%), an aging population, household debt near 90% of GDP, few new-economy companies, and steady foreign selling. If those don't change, the "fair" multiple may now be closer to 13–14x than 16x. In that case you're getting fair value, not a bargain.
3. **Index composition.** The SET is heavy in banks, energy, property, and old-economy conglomerates. Its P/E reflects those sectors' prospects, and they don't deserve high multiples right now.
4. **What caused the drop?** A sentiment or liquidity shock (foreign outflows, politics, global risk-off) tends to reverse. A drop driven by earnings (exports, tourism, credit quality) often doesn't, at least not quickly.
5. **Compare with alternatives.** An earnings yield of 1/13 ≈ 7.7% looks good next to Thai bond yields. It looks less special next to other emerging markets that may be just as cheap and growing faster.

### A practical way to decide
- **Cheap (likely opportunity):** forward EPS stable or rising, the drop driven by flows or sentiment, and valuation also low on P/BV and dividend yield.
- **Value trap (be careful):** EPS forecasts still falling, foreign selling continuing, and no catalyst like policy stimulus, rate cuts, or a tourism or export recovery.

### If you want to act
Don't go all in on one signal. Buying in stages (DCA over 3–6 months) gets you exposure at a discount while protecting you if this turns into a long de-rating. Favor quality, high-dividend names or a low-cost SET50 index fund over speculative small caps. Size the position so a further 10–15% fall wouldn't force you to sell.

**Bottom line:** It's cheap compared with its own history. It's not clearly cheap on fundamentals until you've confirmed that earnings aren't about to fall further. Check forward EPS revisions before deciding.
