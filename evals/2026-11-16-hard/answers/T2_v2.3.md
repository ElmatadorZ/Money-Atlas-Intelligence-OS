**Short answer:** 2x leverage will not reliably get you to 15%. Once you pay interest on the borrowed half and lose some return to volatility drag, the math gives about **15% minus your borrowing rate**. At a realistic borrowing rate that is roughly **10% a year**. In exchange, your volatility doubles to about 20%, and a bad stretch could take **40–50%+** off your money. On these numbers I would not lever 2x to chase 15%.

📍 SITUATION MAP
- **Given:** 8% a year, 10% volatility. Those are *past* results. They do not promise future returns. A lot of 60/40's history comes from decades of falling interest rates, which pushed bond prices up, and that tailwind is not guaranteed to repeat.
- **Assumed:** I read the 8% as a compound annual rate (CAGR). Borrowing cost is **[ASSUMED] 5%**, meaning short-term cash rates of about 4% plus a broker's spread of about 1%. Your actual rate decides the answer, so the table below covers a range.
- **Unknown:** your time horizon, income stability, emergency fund, how you would borrow (margin loan, futures or a leveraged fund), and how much loss you could live through without selling.

📍 FIRST PRINCIPLE BREAKDOWN: the arithmetic
- Unlevered: a CAGR of 8% with 10% vol means an average yearly return of about 8.5% (8% + ½·0.10²).
- At 2x: average return = 2 × 8.5% − 1 × borrow rate = 17% − r.
- Volatility drag at 2x: ½ × (0.20)² = 2%.
- **Compound return at 2x ≈ 15% − r**, with volatility of about 20%.

| Borrow rate | 2x compound return | Volatility |
|---|---|---|
| 0% | 15% | 20% |
| 3% | 12% | 20% |
| 5% [ASSUMED] | **10%** | 20% |
| 6.5% | 8.5% (barely above the unlevered 8%) | 20% |

15% at 2x only works if borrowing is free. With 5% borrowing, the most this portfolio can compound at *any* leverage is about 11% (at around 3.5x), and the risk at that level would be extreme. **15% cannot be reached with leverage on these inputs.**

📍 EXPECTED DRAWDOWN
- **One year, at 2x (5% borrow):** a typical range of about −8% to +32% (12% ± 20%). In a 2-sigma bad year you would be down about −28%.
- **Over 10 years:** the annualised result could plausibly land anywhere from about 4% to 16% (spread ≈ 20%/√10). 10% is the centre of that range. It is not a promise.
- **Peak-to-trough:** suppose the unlevered portfolio falls **[ASSUMED] 25%**, which is in the range of past major 60/40 bear markets. Rebalanced 2x leverage turns that into about 1 − 0.75² ≈ **−44%**, and more like **−45% to −50%** after interest and path effects. A 30% fall becomes about −51% or worse.
- **Margin-call risk:** with a plain 2x margin loan and **[ASSUMED] 25% maintenance**, your equity hits the call level after a portfolio fall of about **33%**. At that point you can be forced to sell at the bottom and lock in the loss permanently.

📍 SCENARIOS
- **Benign:** stocks and bonds stay diversified and rates fall. Trigger: inflation keeps easing and policy rates come down. Result: you compound at roughly 11–13% with bearable swings.
- **Inflation or rate shock (the main risk):** stocks and bonds fall *together* (2022 is the template), and your borrowing cost *rises* at the same moment. Trigger: inflation picks up again. Result: losses on both halves of the portfolio, a higher interest bill and a possible margin call. Leverage is weakest in exactly this regime.
- **Base:** returns come in below the historical 8%. Trigger: today's starting valuations and yields. Result: 2x delivers well under 15%, probably high single digits to about 10%, with twice the swings.

📍 DECISION FRAMEWORK
- **If the real goal is a dollar target** (for example, X by age Y): work out the required return first. Saving more, a longer horizon or a later target usually closes the gap more safely than leverage.
- **If you want more expected return and can stand 20% volatility:** compare 2x 60/40 with simply holding more equities, which needs no borrowing, has no margin call and costs no interest. You give up some diversification. Futures-based leverage usually borrows more cheaply than a margin loan.
- **If you do use leverage:** size it so that a **[ASSUMED] 35–40%** fall in the unlevered portfolio cannot trigger a forced sale. In practice that means well under 2x, for example 1.2–1.4x, with a cash buffer. **How much you lever is the main control you have.**
- **If a 45% drop would make you sell:** do not lever. Selling at the bottom is how leverage turns a temporary loss into a permanent one.

📍 RISK & FAILURE MODE
This analysis stops holding if your borrowing rate is much lower than 5% or if forward returns beat history. It also assumes normal-ish returns. Real crashes have fatter tails, so treat the drawdown estimates as optimistic.

**CONFIDENCE:** HIGH on the leverage arithmetic (15% − r, doubled volatility, about 2x the drawdown), because it follows directly from the formulas. LOW on the actual outcome, because it depends on future returns, the stock–bond correlation and your borrowing rate, none of which can be known from here. | **KEY UNKNOWNS:** your borrowing rate and method, horizon, liquidity buffer, how much loss you can tolerate, and forward 60/40 returns.
