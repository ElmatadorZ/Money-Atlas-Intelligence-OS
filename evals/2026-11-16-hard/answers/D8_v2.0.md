📍 **MARKET STRUCTURE INSIGHT**
Duration alone overstates the loss. It treats the price–yield link as a straight line, but the real curve is convex, so the bond loses less when yields rise (and gains more when they fall). Here convexity gives back about 1 percentage point. With a move as large as 150 bp, that correction matters.

📍 **KEY RISK: what could make this estimate wrong**
- **How convexity is quoted.** The result above assumes the common textbook form, where convexity is used with the ½ factor. Some data vendors quote convexity already scaled, or use the formula without the ½. On that basis the adjustment would be +2.03% and the estimate about −10.73%. Check how your source defines convexity before you rely on the number.
- **It's still an approximation.** A second-order Taylor estimate leaves some error on a 150 bp shift. For an exact figure, reprice the bond from its cash flows at the new yield. That needs the coupon, the current yield and the exact maturity, none of which were given.
- **It assumes a parallel shift.** The estimate assumes the whole yield curve moves up by the same amount. If the curve steepens or flattens instead, the real loss could differ.
- **It assumes no embedded options.** If the bond is callable or putable, use effective duration and effective convexity. A callable bond can even have negative convexity.
